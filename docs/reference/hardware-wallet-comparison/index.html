Hardware wallet comparison

For a first Bitcoin hardware wallet in October 2026, the BitBox02 Nova (€175) and the Trezor Safe 5 ($129) are the easiest devices to use safely. For security you can verify yourself rather than take on trust, the Blockstream Jade Plus ($149) signs over QR codes without a cable and is fully open source. Our favourite is the SeedSigner, a stateless signer you build yourself, for anyone comfortable with the assembly; it is not a beginner's device. Coldcard is not recommended after the 2026 entropy incident, and Ledger and Bitkey are not recommended because their critical parts cannot be verified. Which device you buy matters less than three habits: supply your own dice entropy when the seed is created, add a passphrase, and split keys across vendors once the balance is significant.

This comparison weighs devices differently from most. In July 2026, attackers drained about $116 million from wallets whose seeds had been generated on Coldcards running firmware with a five-year-old flaw: the devices were air-gapped and worked as designed, but their seeds were guessable. What happened and who is affected is the full account. Since then, whether you can check a device's randomness, and how its maker behaves when something goes wrong, count for as much as its screen and chips.

In the dice column, "on the device" means you enter dice rolls on the device itself; "vendor procedure" means the maker documents a way to choose the words yourself and enter them; "import only" means you make the seed with dice yourself and restore it onto the device, which works on every device here except Bitkey.

How should you choose? Practices before devices​

The 2026 incident made one thing measurable: the practices around a device protected people better than the device itself. Coldcard owners who had used dice entropy, a strong passphrase or multisig lost nothing; owners who trusted the device's defaults lost everything. Decide on the practices first:

  1. Supply your own entropy. A seed you make yourself with dice and then restore onto the device works with every device we recommend, and it removes the exact failure that cost Coldcard users $116 million. The Keystone 3 Pro and SeedSigner also take dice rolls on the device, and BitBox, Blockstream and Foundation document procedures of their own; the entropy table below lists each.
  2. Add a passphrase for any balance you would miss. In July 2026 it was the difference between drained and untouched.
  3. Use multisig across vendors for significant holdings, so that no single vendor's mistake can reach your funds.

With those in place, the choice of device comes down to how easy it makes verification (screen size, buttons, QR or USB), how much you trust its supply chain, and how its maker has behaved when things went wrong.

Blockstream Jade and Jade Plus​

The Jade and Jade Plus are the verifiable-security pick: fully open source, able to sign over QR codes, and designed so the device cannot leak key material. Our Jade Plus setup guide sets one up air-gapped on words chosen with dice.

AspectDetails
PriceJade $79; Jade Plus $149 in plastic, $169 in metal
ScreenColour LCD (the Plus has a larger screen and a better camera)
ConnectivityUSB, Bluetooth, and a camera for QR air-gapping
Open sourceYes, fully
Secure elementNo: a virtual "blind oracle" model
Dice entropyNot on the device. Blockstream documents dice methods for choosing the words, and the Jade calculates valid last words; or restore a seed you made with dice. By default it mixes several entropy sources, and wallet apps can add their own
CoinsBitcoin and Liquid
StrengthsA real QR air-gap at a low price, anti-exfiltration signing, several entropy sources, an exemplary response to the 2026 incident
WeaknessesNo hardware secure element, so the oracle model needs understanding; the camera workflow is slower than USB
Best forSecurity-focused users on any budget; the Plus's better camera makes it the nicer daily device

The Jade differs from most devices in two ways that both proved wise in 2026. Its anti-exfiltration signing protocol stops a malicious device from leaking key material through its signatures. And instead of one random number generator it mixes several entropy sources, a design Blockstream documented in detail within hours of the Coldcard news, together with a migration guide for affected users.

The trade-off is the missing secure element. Jade's "blind oracle" splits PIN protection with a Blockstream server, or one you host yourself, that limits how many PIN guesses a thief can make. It is a sound model, but understand it before relying on it.

In April 2026 Blockstream added the Jade Core ($99), which has no camera and therefore no QR air-gap. We have not reviewed it.

SeedSigner & Krux (DIY)​

SeedSigner and Krux are stateless signers you assemble yourself from commodity parts. SeedSigner is our favourite signing device, and the SeedSigner section covers why, how to build one and how to use it; it is not the right choice for most beginners, for the reasons given there.

AspectDetails
PriceSeedSigner about $50-80 in parts; Krux about $30-80 (K210-based devices)
ScreenSmall LCD (SeedSigner: 1.3-inch, with a camera for QR codes)
ConnectivityQR codes only, fully air-gapped
Open sourceYes, fully, community-built and reproducible
Secure elementNone: the device stores nothing, so there is nothing to protect
Dice entropyRequired: you bring the entropy
Bitcoin-onlyYes, by design
Best forTechnical users, multisig quorums, and anyone who has built a coreboot laptop and enjoyed it

SeedSigner (built on a Raspberry Pi Zero) and Krux take the 2026 lesson furthest: the device never stores a seed. You bring the seed to each signing session, usually as a SeedQR or by typing it in, and the device forgets everything when it is switched off. Because you generate the seed yourself (with dice, and verified), there is no vendor random number generator to trust, and because the parts are commodity hardware, there is no wallet-specific supply chain to intercept. The cost is convenience: every signing session takes longer, and keeping the seed safe is entirely on you. Start with the build guide.

Coldcard Mk5 & Q​

Coldcard is not currently recommended for new purchases. For years it was this site's security pick. Then came the entropy incident: a 2021 firmware bug silently replaced hardware randomness with a predictable software generator, about $116 million was stolen from more than 5,200 wallets, and, decisively for us, a developer had warned Coinkite about that exact code in May 2025 and been dismissed. As of October 2026, no compensation has been offered.

The current hardware (Mk5 $219, Q $319 at the store's October 2026 prices) is capable, the fixed firmware has been independently reviewed, and seeds generated with dice rolls were never affected. If you already own one, update the firmware, check whether your seed needs moving, and make any new seed with dice. For new buyers, we do not recommend rewarding a vendor whose handling of a credible warning cost its users nine figures, at least until a longer record rebuilds that trust.

Ledger​

Ledger devices (Nano S Plus, Nano X and Nano Gen5, Flex, Stax; $69 to $399 as of October 2026) have strong secure elements and came through 2026 without an entropy incident; the company was quick to point to its certified hardware random number generator. Our concerns are unchanged. The secure element firmware is closed source; the 2023 Ledger Recover service showed the firmware can export seed material, split into shares; and the 2020 customer-data breach exposed buyers to years of phishing. You cannot verify the parts that matter most, and 2026 showed how much silent trust that asks for. Ledger suits people committed to its multi-coin ecosystem; for bitcoin self-custody, the open devices above are stronger choices.

Bitkey​

Bitkey, from Block, uses a different trust model entirely, and one you cannot verify.

AspectDetails
Price$250 (August 2026; the 2026 model with a touchscreen; the original was $150)
ScreenTouchscreen for checking transactions on the device (2026 model)
ConnectivityBluetooth, with a companion phone app; no air-gap
Open sourcePartly: source is published, but it cannot be built, because the fingerprint library is closed
Secure elementYes, a secure enclave, unlocked by fingerprint
Dice entropyNo; there is no seed phrase shown to the user at all
Bitcoin-onlyYes

Bitkey ships as a 2-of-3 multisig by default (your phone, the device and a recovery server held by Block), with no seed phrase shown to the user and a fingerprint instead of a PIN. It was untouched by the 2026 incident, since by design no single random number generator failure can spend funds, and its communication that night was among the best.

We still do not recommend it, because you cannot verify it. The published firmware cannot be reproduced by outsiders because the proprietary fingerprint-matching library is missing from the repository; WalletScrutiny rates it "nosource". There is no seed phrase to check, no dice entropy to supply, and most recovery paths run through Block's servers. That is the "trust the vendor" posture that cost Coldcard users nine figures; Block has a clean record, but so did Coinkite until July 2026.

Bitkey's fair niche is someone leaving an exchange who would otherwise never hold their own keys: its 2-of-3 design with an Emergency Exit Kit is far better than custody by an exchange. If you can follow a setup guide, a BitBox02 Nova or Trezor Safe 5 gives you a portable, standard BIP39 seed that you can verify and recover anywhere.

Entropy: who lets you verify?​

This table is the lesson of 2026: where the randomness in your seed comes from, and how you can check it. As of October 2026 only a few devices take dice rolls on the device; every device except Bitkey can take a seed you made yourself from dice, by restoring it.

Whatever you buy, a device could in theory ignore your dice. The check that catches it is re-deriving the seed from the same rolls independently, which the dice guide shows how to do.

Common questions​

What is the best Bitcoin hardware wallet in 2026?​

There is no single best device, and after the 2026 Coldcard entropy incident the practices matter more than the hardware. For beginners, the Trezor Safe 5 ($129) or the BitBox02 Nova (€175) is the easiest to use safely. For verifiable security on a budget, the Blockstream Jade Plus ($149) signs over QR codes. SeedSigner is our favourite for people comfortable building their own device, though it is not for beginners. Whichever you choose, supply your own dice entropy when the seed is created, add a passphrase, and use multisig across vendors for significant holdings.

Is Coldcard still safe to use in 2026?​

We do not currently recommend Coldcard. A build error meant Coldcards generated seeds with a software pseudo-random generator instead of the hardware random number generator between March 2021 and July 2026, and roughly 1,816 BTC was drained from more than 5,200 addresses. Coinkite shipped fixes quickly once the draining began, but it had dismissed a developer's warning about the same code in May 2025. The devices are safe to use with a seed generated from dice or elsewhere, but we point new buyers to other devices.

Do you need a secure element in a hardware wallet?​

Not necessarily. A secure element resists someone physically extracting the seed from a stolen device, which matters if theft is part of your threat model. Devices without one, such as the Blockstream Jade Plus, use a blind oracle design instead and are fully open source and verifiable. The 2026 Coldcard incident is the reminder that a secure element does not help if the seed was weak to begin with.

Should you use dice to generate your Bitcoin seed phrase?​

Yes, for any amount you would be upset to lose. Every hardware wallet we recommend can take a seed you made from dice, by restoring it, and a few take the rolls directly. Coldcard owners who used dice lost nothing in the 2026 incident, while owners who trusted the device's default lost everything. Dice remove your dependence on the vendor's random number generator being correct.
