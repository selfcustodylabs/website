Start here: Bitcoin self-custody

Self-custody means holding the private keys to your own bitcoin, so that no exchange, bank or company can freeze it, lose it or spend it. This site shows how to do that safely: understanding what you are protecting against, making a seed phrase, setting up a hardware wallet, testing the backup, and then adding a passphrase, multisig or your own node when the amount justifies it. If your bitcoin is on an exchange today, the hardware wallet setup guide is the step that matters most; if you are new to bitcoin, read the pages below in order.

What is self-custody?​

Self-custody is holding bitcoin by controlling its private keys yourself, instead of holding a claim on bitcoin that an exchange keeps for you. An exchange balance is the company's promise to pay you bitcoin when you ask. On the blockchain, the bitcoin belongs to whoever controls the keys, and on an exchange that is the exchange.

Exchange accountSelf-custody
Who holds the keysThe exchangeYou
Can the funds be frozen?Yes, by the company or by a court order served on itNo
Withdrawing or sendingNeeds the company's approvalNeeds nobody's approval
If the company fails or is hackedYou become one of its creditorsYour bitcoin is unaffected
If you lose your password or backupSupport can reset the passwordThe bitcoin is gone

The last row is the price of self-custody: there is no password reset and no way to reverse a payment. What is self-custody? covers it in full, including the exchange failures that made the case for it.

What can go wrong with self-custody?​

Self-custody moves the risk from a company to the way you handle your seed phrase, the 12 or 24 words that back up the wallet. The mistakes that lose bitcoin are permanent:

  • Losing the seed phrase and the device together. Nobody can restore the wallet.
  • Showing the seed phrase to anyone, including "support staff" or a website that asks for it. Whoever has the words can spend the bitcoin.
  • Trusting a backup that was never tested. A miswritten word is discovered only when the device fails, which is too late.

The setup guides are written to prevent all three, and backup verification exists for the last one.

How much security do you need?​

The right setup depends on how much you hold, who might come after it, and how much technical work you are willing to do. Most people need one hardware wallet with a tested backup; the rest is for larger amounts or specific threats.

If you are protectingStart with
An amount you could lose without real harmA software wallet on your phone
Savings you would missA hardware wallet
A life-changing amountMultisig, with keys on two or three devices kept in different places
If the threat isAdd
Malware, phishing or an exchange failingNothing: a hardware wallet covers these
Someone targeting you personallyAir-gapped signing, multisig, and not telling people what you hold
A government or an attacker with similar resourcesAll of the above, plus operational security

If you are not technical, start with one hardware wallet and add layers as you learn; nothing on the advanced pages needs doing on the first day. Assess your threat model works through these questions in detail.

For large holdings or a higher threat level. Several days to a few weeks.

  2. Multisig: remove the single point of failure
  3. Air-gapped wallets: sign on a device that never touches a network
  4. CoinJoin: break the link between your coins' past and present

What principles hold across every guide?​

Verify instead of trusting​

Check every receive address on the hardware wallet's own screen, not on the computer's. Check firmware signatures before installing. Compare what this site says with the vendor's documentation and other sources; we get things wrong too.

Go slowly​

Mistakes with bitcoin are usually permanent, and the deadlines that push people into them are usually invented. Read a guide through before starting it, send a small test amount first, and treat anyone hurrying you as a warning sign.

Assume your computer is compromised​

A hardware wallet is designed for the case where the computer or phone it connects to is controlled by an attacker: the device shows the transaction on its own screen and signs it there. That protection only holds if the seed phrase never touches a connected device, so never type it, photograph it or store it in a password manager.

Avoid single points of failure​

One backup in one place can burn, flood or be found. Backups in separate places, and for larger amounts multisig, mean that no single loss empties the wallet.

Practise recovery​

A backup you have never restored from is untested. Restore it once before depositing anything significant, using the backup verification guide.
